Audiobooks too loud at night? Fall asleep without waking your partner

A phone's lowest volume step is loud enough to hear from across a room, which is exactly the wrong loudness for something meant to be almost inaudible next to someone sleeping.

Updated October 4, 2026 · 5 min read

Falling asleep to a podcast or an audiobook only works if it's quiet enough not to become the thing that keeps someone else awake instead. For a lot of phones and tablets, the lowest non-mute volume step doesn't get there. It's calibrated for normal daytime listening, not for something meant to fade into the background of a dark, quiet bedroom.

This comes down to how Android's volume system is built: the lowest step above mute is a fixed fraction of the device's maximum output, not a fixed loudness. There's no separate "bedtime" calibration, and no setting in stock Android that changes where that floor sits, regardless of what's actually playing through it.

The short answer

Android's lowest volume step is a fixed fraction of your phone's maximum, and no setting moves it. A sleep timer ends playback and sleep headphones keep the sound near you, but neither lowers that floor. To listen quietly enough not to wake your partner, the output has to be reduced below the system's own minimum, which is the last row here.

Why the usual advice doesn't fix this

The most common suggestion is an app that adds more volume steps. It helps a little and then stops. Android places each step on a fixed loudness curve by its position in the range, so the first step of 100 does land lower than the first step of 15, worth roughly 5 dB on one configuration we measured. But the curve has a fixed bottom, and no step count reaches past it. If the quietest level your phone can produce is still too loud for a shared bedroom, more steps will not get you under it.

What actually gets you quieter

A volume reduction applied underneath the existing volume range, rather than more clicks within it, is what actually changes the floor. You can also get there by re-encoding a file to be quieter before you play it, which genuinely works but has to be done per file and is no help for streaming. FAQ

Does the overlay turn itself off automatically overnight?
No, it stays exactly at the level you set until you change it or close it, there's no automatic timeout. If you want it off by morning, closing it with one tap or using the Quick Settings tile before you fall asleep is the way to do that.
Does running it all night drain the battery noticeably?
The app itself does very little while it runs: it keeps one audio effect on the phone's output and waits for you. That effect works on the audio that is already playing, so it adds a small amount of processing on top of playback. We have not measured the battery cost separately.
Will it keep working if I lock the phone screen?
Yes. It runs as a foreground service specifically so the volume reduction and playback continue normally with the screen off or locked, the same way any other audio app keeps playing after you lock your phone.
Will my morning alarm be quieter too?
Yes, if the dial is still below the line when the alarm rings. The quiet steps lower media, ringtones, notifications and alarms together. The mute button is different: it silences media only and leaves alarms alone. If you wake up to your phone's alarm, bring the dial back to the line or close it when you finish listening, or check once that your alarm is still loud enough at the step you use.
What if someone calls during the night?
The ringtone follows the quiet step. The call itself does not: Android plays regular phone calls on a separate path that no app can lower, so a regular call is at your phone's normal call volume.
