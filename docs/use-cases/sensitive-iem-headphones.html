IEMs too loud at the lowest volume? A quieter floor for sensitive ears

A phone's minimum volume step is set for average headphones. In-ear monitors and bone conduction headphones aren't average, and that gap is exactly why the lowest setting still feels too loud.

Updated October 4, 2026 · 4 min read

In-ear monitors are usually far more sensitive than the earbuds or headphones a phone's volume curve was tuned around, often specified in decibels of output per milliwatt of input rather than the rougher "loud/quiet" range typical consumer earbuds are designed for. Bone conduction headphones have a different problem: at low volumes the vibration itself can feel uncomfortable in a way that's separate from perceived loudness. Both end up in the same place: the phone's lowest non-mute step is genuinely too much.

This isn't a flaw specific to your headphones. Android's volume steps are calibrated against the device's own maximum output level, not against whatever is actually plugged in. The lowest step is a fixed fraction of that maximum, and no amount of headphone sensitivity is factored into where that floor sits.

The actual fix

Granular Volume adds real volume steps below the hardware minimum, applied to Android's own output before it reaches your headphones. It works on the phone speaker and over Bluetooth on the phones we have tested; a 3.5 mm jack should behave the same, and USB-C adapters and DACs are not verified, so check yours during the seven free days.

Why "more precise" volume apps don't solve this

The most common suggestion for this problem is an app that adds more volume steps: a hundred clicks instead of fifteen. That does two things: it smooths the transitions, and it lowers the first step a little, because Android places each step on a fixed loudness curve according to its position in the range. On one configuration we measured, going from 15 steps to 100 bought about 5 dB. Then it stops, because the curve has a fixed bottom that no step count passes. On drivers sensitive enough that the stock first step is already too loud, a few dB often is not the gap you need to close.

What actually changes the floor

A volume reduction applied through Android's own audio framework, rather than a remapped step count, genuinely produces output below what the hardware's own minimum step allows. It's a clean gain-reduction stage, not an equalizer or a compressor, so it doesn't reshape the signal your headphones receive, it just turns all of it down further than the phone's own slider is able to.

  • Works through the phone's speaker and Bluetooth on the phones we have tested; a 3.5 mm jack should work the same way. USB-C DACs are not verified yet, so check yours during the free days.
  • Doesn't take over your physical volume buttons. The slider covers your normal range too, and adds the quiet headroom underneath it.
  • No equalizer curve, no compression, so it doesn't change the tonal balance you're used to, only the overall level.

FAQ

Does this work with wired IEMs, a 3.5mm jack or a USB-C DAC?
The phone's speaker and Bluetooth headphones work on the phones we have tested. A 3.5 mm headphone jack normally uses the same main output, so it should work too, though we have not measured it separately. USB audio, such as USB-C adapters and DACs, has not been verified: USB output can run on a separate path, and the one USB route we tested, a car connected over USB, was not reached. Whatever you use, check it on yours during the seven free days.
Does it work with Bluetooth IEMs and TWS earbuds?
In most cases, yes: the reduction is applied to Android's output before the signal is sent to the earbuds. On a small number of phone and headphone combinations the quiet steps have no effect over Bluetooth, so check your own pair during the seven free days.
Does going that low introduce distortion or hiss at very low levels?
It does not add hiss of its own. It's a clean gain-reduction stage rather than a compressor or limiter, so it doesn't reshape the signal. One thing it cannot do, and this matters if hiss is your actual problem: the reduction happens in software, before the signal reaches your DAC and amplifier, so it cannot lower a hiss those stages are producing. Turning the music down that way leaves that background noise where it was, which makes it relatively easier to hear. If the noise floor is what bothers you rather than the loudness, a passive inline volume control on the cable is what addresses that, because it works after the amplifier. This app is for the case where the sound is simply too loud at its lowest step.
